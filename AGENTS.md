# Repository Guidelines

## Project Structure & Module Organization
This Maven project keeps code under `src/main/kotlin/rayTraceTypescript`, split into domain-focused packages such as `materials`, `objects`, and `utils`. The entry point is `Main.kt`, which wires up the `Camera`, scene configuration, and invokes `render`, producing `image.ppm` in the repo root. Build artifacts land in `target/`, and `pom.xml` pins Kotlin 2.2.21 plus the Exec plugin that targets `rayTraceTypescript.MainKt`. Keep any future assets (sample renders, textures) in a dedicated top-level folder so they do not pollute `target/`.

## Rendering Backends
Ray tracing runs on NVIDIA GPUs through CUDA (LWJGL's `lwjgl-cuda`: the driver API from `nvcuda.dll`, which the driver installs, and NVRTC from `native/cuda/windows-x64`, see its README - no CUDA Toolkit needed). The kernel is CUDA C++ in `src/main/resources/kernels/raytracer.cu`, compiled at run time by `gpu/CudaContext` for the card found with the scene's features switched in. `Camera.initialize()` produces the shared `CameraSetup`, `gpu/SceneFlattener` lays the scene out as flat buffers, and the kernel does sampling, materials and textures; the CPU only writes the PPM through `render/PpmWriter`. There is no CPU fallback: without a CUDA device a render fails with the reason.

Two engines find the hits, behind the `gpu/GpuSession` interface. `gpu/CudaSession` runs the kernel as it is, with its own BVH traversal (sm_61 on a GTX 1070). `gpu/optix/OptixSession` builds the same file with `-DRT_OPTIX=1` as an OptiX program: OptiX builds and searches the tree - on an RTX card its RT cores do - and only `world_hit` and the entry point differ, the path loop, materials, textures and light sampling being shared text. OptiX has no binding here: `gpu/optix/OptixApi` takes the table of functions the driver's `nvoptix.dll` hands out and calls them through libffi (`utils/NativeFunction`), and `gpu/optix/OptixLayout` asks the compiler how OptiX's structures are laid out, with a probe kernel built against the SDK headers - which are NVIDIA's and fetched, not committed (`native/optix/README.md`; OptiX 9.0, ABI 105, a driver of the 570 series or newer). On an RTX 2060 OptiX traces Yennefer 3.2 times as fast as the CUDA kernel (256 spp: 2.06 s vs 6.53 s); on a GTX 1070, which has no RT cores, it is the slower of the two (17.1 s vs 11.75 s). It takes every scene the CUDA kernel does. Spheres are custom primitives: a tree of their own over their boxes (a moving sphere's covering its whole path, an inside-out one's by the size of its radius), whose intersection program calls the kernel's `sphere_t`; the ray's time rides in a seventh payload value only in a scene with moving spheres (`-DOPTIX_RAY_TIME`), since declared everywhere it slowed Yennefer by 4%. Fog is tested after OptiX's search: `world_hit` runs the kernel's `medium_t` over each volume up to the nearest surface. On the RTX 2060 at 1080 wide and 256 spp, OptiX against the CUDA kernel: `model` with Yennefer on its sphere floor 1.02 s vs 3.10 s, `final-week` 2.10 vs 4.60, `cornell-smoke` 1.90 vs 3.52, `perlin-spheres` 0.60 vs 0.62. Rays end at 1e16 rather than infinity, which OptiX's validation rejects. `-Drt.optix.include` / `OPTIX_INCLUDE` and `-Drt.optix.dll` / `OPTIX_DLL` point at other headers and another library, `-Drt.optix.log=1..4` sets how much OptiX reports, `-Drt.optix.validate` has it check every call.

One card traces: the best one - the card OptiX can use, then the newest architecture - or the one `-Drt.gpu=<number or piece of its name>` names (`gpu/GpuChoice`). `-Drt.backend` is `auto` (the default), `optix` or `cuda`: `auto` is OptiX on a card with RT cores, where the headers and the library are there, and the CUDA kernel elsewhere; `gpu/SwitchingSession` takes such a card to the CUDA kernel for a scene OptiX does not trace (now only one of nothing but fog, which leaves OptiX no tree). The same card denoises (see *Rendering Output*); any other card in the machine stays unused, and a GTX 1070 is no longer used at all (removed 2026-11-17: it denoised a full-size image in 0.31 s against 0.07 s on the RTX 2060, and gained 0.04 s a film frame at best). Sharing one image's samples between cards was tried and removed (2026-11-17): the GTX 1070 on the CUDA kernel beside the RTX 2060 on OptiX took Yennefer at 256 spp from 2.11 s to 1.91 s, added nothing to the viewer at 1080x1920, and slowed upscaled films (0.31 s a frame against 0.19), the per-frame scene copy for its kernel costing more than it traced. With the GTX 10 card out of the machine the driver is free to be newer than 581.57; one card, one driver. The hidden OpenGL window that keeps the driver from clocking the card down (`GpuKeepAwake`, `-Drt.keepawake=off`) stays, as it was: on the RTX 2060 it made no measurable difference (a 1024-spp Yennefer in 13.9-14.0 s without it, 14.3 s with; the viewer's quick look, a bursty load, sits at 420-690 MHz in P3 either way), so it may well be dead weight now that the GTX 10 card it was written for is out of use.

`-Drt.nvrtc=<dir>` / `NVRTC_DIR` point at another NVRTC 12.x (e.g. a toolkit's `bin`), `-Drt.cuda.options` adds compiler flags and `-Drt.cuda.log` shows what the compiler said. The kernel began as a port of the OpenCL kernel that preceded it (last in commit `1b65ee9`); on a GTX 1070 it traces about 15% slower than that one did (Yennefer at 256 spp: 11.7 s of GPU time vs 10.1 s) with near-identical machine code, and Nsight Compute does not support Pascal, so the cause is unknown. The CPU path tracer that preceded both is gone too; its renders of the comparison scenes are kept as test references (`src/test/resources/cpu-reference`, `reference-image*.ppm.gz`). The scene classes keep their CPU `hit` code, which the viewer's picking and placement use. Because the GPU draws its random numbers per pixel and sample, renders match those references in geometry, shading and brightness but carry a different noise pattern - compare images statistically (as `GpuCpuComparisonTest` does), never byte for byte. Instances: the CPU classes transform rays at traversal time, the flattener folds the same rigid transform into the primitives.

## Meshes and 3D File Formats
`objects/MeshLoader` reads a model file into a `Mesh` - a flat triangle soup - picking a parser in `objects/formats` by extension: STL (binary and ASCII, told apart by file size rather than the `solid` keyword), OBJ, PLY (ASCII and both binary byte orders), OFF, glTF 2.0 (`.gltf` with external or data-URI buffers, and packed `.glb`), and 3MF. Only geometry is read; materials come from the scene. Faces with more than three corners are fanned into triangles, glTF node transforms are baked into world space, and glTF needs a JSON reader that `utils/Json` provides, because the project has no JSON dependency. `Mesh.toHittables` turns each facet into a `Triangle` - Moller-Trumbore intersection, barycentric u/v, and a normal taken from the winding rather than from the file, since mesh files frequently carry wrong ones. A model from a file is shaded smooth (`Mesh.smooth`, set by `MeshLoader`; `-Drt.smooth=off` keeps the facets): `Mesh.smoothNormals` welds corners that are bit for bit the same point (-0 taken as +0) and gives each the area-weighted sum of its facets' normals, as StlViewer does; the kernel blends a facet's three corner normals by the hit's barycentric coordinates (`smooth_normal`), takes the blend only when it leans the same way as the facet, and keeps the facet's own normal (`gnormal`) for what is in front and behind - a reflection or a bounce under the true surface ends the path. The CUDA layout carries the normals turned and in leaf order (`SceneBuffers.triangleNormals`, `MeshBlock.cornerNormals`), the OptiX one in each mesh's own space, turned per hit; `HAS_SMOOTH` keeps the code out of scenes without a smooth mesh. Meshes made in code, such as the tests' octahedrons, stay flat. Texture coordinates come from an OBJ's `vt` (`Mesh.uvs`, 6 floats a triangle, NaN first for a face without) and a colour image from its .mtl's `map_Kd` (`MeshMaterial.texture`, one `ImageTexture` per file however many materials name it, on matte surfaces only); the CUDA layout carries them in leaf order (`SceneBuffers.triangleUvs`, `MeshBlock.cornerUvs`), the OptiX one per mesh in its own order (`Instance.uvs`), and the kernel blends the corners' by the hit's barycentric coordinates and wraps them (`mesh_uv`, `HAS_UV`). A model file's materials go further than the books' (`MeshMaterial`): a metal (`Pm`) is physically based - coloured texel by texel by its `map_Kd`, GGX microfacets of alpha = roughness squared sampled in proportion to D(h)cos, weighed by Schlick's Fresnel and Smith's shadowing (`Metal.fresnel`; the books' metals keep their fuzz and flat tint), its roughness a texel at a time from `map_Pr` (read as data, `RtwImage.load(data = true)`); `map_Ke` makes a surface glow in places on top of what it reflects, times `Ke` (`glow`); `norm` / `map_Bump` is a tangent-space normal map, DirectX-style, its tangents worked out per hit from the triangle's edges and texture coordinates in both engines (`uv_tangents`, `HAS_NORMALMAP`). The material table holds four ints (kind, colour texture, glow texture, roughness texture) and four floats (fuzz, refractive index or Fresnel switch, glow scale, normal map + 1) a material. Glass (`d` below 0.5, `Ni`) may be coloured through its body: `Tf r g b [distance]` is what is left of the light after that distance through it (one model unit without the fourth number, which the format lacks), Beer-Lambert (`Dielectric.tint`), applied as a ray leaves the glass by the length it went inside (`rec->t` times its direction's length), so thick glass is darker than thin. Not read yet: alpha cut-outs (leaves render as solid cards), a glossy layer on non-metals (polished floors), textures on glass. `-Drt.scene=file` renders a whole scene from one file at its authored size under the sky and a sun (`-Drt.sun=x,y,z|off`, `-Drt.camera.from|at|vfov`; lamps from a `<model>.lights` file beside it, a line `point x y z r g b watts [radius]` each, a ball of 0.1 units or that radius - a larger one softens shadows and caustics and clears their noise); Amazon Lumberyard Bistro (CC BY 4.0, NVIDIA ORCA) was converted for it from FBX with Blender, its DDS colour textures saved as PNGs of at most 1024 px: the exterior, 2.83 M triangles and 132 textures, renders at 1920x1080 and 256 spp in 5.3 s through OptiX. Every sample is also capped at `Camera.fireflyClamp` (its brightest channel; 16, StlViewer's cap, `-Drt.firefly=<n>|off`; studio-model, whose exposure is in its lamps, uses 16 times that exposure); the tests against the stored CPU renders keep every sample. The cap flattens caustics as the path tracer alone finds them - a path off a matte surface through glass or off a mirror that happens to hit an area light - so brute-force caustic renders take `-Drt.firefly=off`, a light of some size (a sun of 0.02-0.03 rad, a lamp of 1.8 cm at 90 cm) and many samples: `models/caustics` (untracked; `gen.mjs` builds a fluted tumbler under a shaded spot, an amber carafe and glass, a gold ring - lookalikes of vk_gltf_renderer's photon-mapped caustics screenshots, whose generator is not public; the props stand 0.2 mm above the floor, since a face in the floor's own plane leaves photons and camera paths to disagree on which they meet) renders at 32768 spp in 56-148 s at 1024-1600 wide on the RTX 2060, denoised clean; at 2048 spp the caustics are still salt noise.

`-Drt.caustics=on` adds photon mapping instead (`gpu/CausticMap`, after vk_gltf_renderer's `docs/caustics.md`, gather only): before each image launch the kernel's photon pass (AOV_MODE 5, `photon_shoot`, a thread a photon in both engines) shoots photons from the scene's light list - a light picked by the light it sends the casters' way, aimed at up to 8 spheres round the casters' faces (built face by face, a model file being one mesh with its floor), the density the mixture over the aims' cones - through the casters (glass, and metal of fuzz up to 0.04: `is_caster`, the one test both sides make) to the first Lambertian surface, where it is kept; `kernels/caustics_grid.cu` sorts the landed ones into a hashed grid (count, prefix sum, scatter), and the camera paths gather them at the first surface they reach that is not a caster (a disc of the launch's radius, on the surface's plane and side, Epanechnikov-weighted), while the path tracer drops a lamp it finds from there through casters (`causticState`), so each path of light counts once. The gathered light is not firefly-clamped, so caustics keep the default clamp. Each launch gets its own map: a quarter of the pixels in photons per sample (`-Drt.caustics.photons`), at most 2 M a launch (`GpuSession.samplesPerLaunch` keeps launches that small), the disc 3 pixels wide at the casters (`-Drt.caustics.radius` in scene units) and shrinking as k^(-1/6) to a third of that; `-Drt.caustics.log` prints how many photons landed (9-18 % in the three scenes). Off by default: without it the kernels build as before. Photons also stop in fog (`ConstantMedium`) past a caster: kept where they stopped, their flux divided by the fog's density (a photon stops in a stretch ds with chance density times ds, so the flux kept in a volume is density times fluence times volume), and a camera path whose first event that is not a caster is in fog gathers them in a ball of the same radius, Epanechnikov-weighted over 8 pi r^3 / 15, times albedo / (4 pi) (`caustic_gather_volume`; the slot's eighth float tells fog photons from surface ones, and each gather skips the other kind). That is the beam a lens or a collimator sends through smoke, which camera paths alone would almost never find. Smoke under a glass slab comes out at 0.884 with photons against 0.886 by brute force at 32768 spp, in both engines (`CausticsTest`). The sky and glowing surfaces that are not on the light list keep their caustics path traced. Measured on the RTX 2060 through OptiX: 1024 spp with photons take 6.6-12.5 s where 2048 spp of brute force take 3.8-9.5 s, and come out with the caustics clean - the tumbler's fluted web, which brute force shows as specks even at 8192 spp - the floor under a glass slab within 6 % of brute force (`CausticsTest`, both engines, which also agree with each other: 36.9 dB on the amber scene). Against 32768-spp brute force the amber scene's caustics match to half a level in 8 bits; on the tumbler the patch at the end of its shadow comes out 13-15 % below light-sampled brute force, but within 0-4 % of brute force without light sampling (`-Drt.lights=off`, 262144 spp, the cup under a 0.02-rad sun). That points at the path tracer's own light sampling of small sphere lights: on the open floor it gives 0.118-0.122 where the sun's irradiance works out to 0.116 by hand and BSDF sampling alone gives 0.114-0.117 - a few per cent too bright, more through glass; not yet tracked down (a far, large sphere such as the sun strains `sphere_t`'s float precision near its rim, which would make `light_pdf_value` miss directions `light_random` drew). The floor seen through thick glass also needs depth: at the default 8 bounces brute force loses up to half of it, so compare at `-Drt.depth=32`.

Model files agree on no units or origin, so `standingOnFloor()` is the default placement: the model is centred over the origin in x/z, rests on y=0 - the top of the ground sphere every scene uses - and is scaled to `DEFAULT_TARGET_HEIGHT`; pass `targetHeight = null` to keep the authored size. `BookScenes.modelWorld(path, material)` assembles that default scene (checkered ground + model) and always wraps it in a `BvhNode`. Run it with `mvn exec:java -Drt.scene=model -Drt.model=<path>`; scenes live in `scenes/BookScenes` and are picked by name, and the path also comes from `MODEL_FILE` or `./model.stl`. Triangles get their own leaf kind in the GPU buffers (`SceneBuffers.LEAF_TRIANGLE`) and their bounding boxes are padded through `Aabb.padded()`, since a facet in an axis-aligned plane would otherwise have a box no ray can enter.

`-Drt.infill=threads|pyramids|diamonds|sponge` fills the model of `model`, `studio-model` or `cornell-model` and leaves the model itself out: only the filling is rendered (`objects/MeshFill`, wired in by `BookScenes.filling`). `SolidGrid` voxelizes the mesh (`-Drt.infill.res`, 256 cells along its longest side; every triangle marks the cells it crosses, the outside is flooded from the grid's border - not ray parity, which breaks on the unclosed STLs the models repository is full of - and if the flood gets in through a hole the marks are thickened by a cell, up to twice, and the outside grown back as much), then an exact distance transform gives each inside cell its depth. Things go only where they fit whole, no deeper than `-Drt.infill.shell` (a fraction of the model's height; 4 times the size by default, since nothing deeper shows), never overlapping, the same `-Drt.infill.seed` giving the same filling. *threads* (the look of a reference image of bead chains swirling round a ball): beads along streamlines of a curl-noise flow (`-Drt.infill.swirl` swirls across the height, 3), the part across the surface taken out and a pull back to the starting depth, so a chain runs along the shape in its own layer; a chain stops where it would touch another or leave, one radius a chain, 1.5 radii apart, tapering over its last 6 beads. Thick chains go first, then thinner ones (sizes 1.35 times apart from `size`, 0.012, down to a quarter of it), each seeded touching the surface (1-1.3 of its radii under it): seeded anywhere in the shell, the thin ones laid later ran over the thick and buried them. *pyramids*: square pyramids packed largest first in 7 sizes spread 15 % either way, from `-Drt.infill.size` (the largest radius, a fraction of the height; 0.025) down to `-Drt.infill.smallest` of it (0.3), each size into the gaps the larger left, through the whole shell and inside the outline, so layers of them leave no gaps (packed near the surface in a wider spread of sizes, larger ones standing out of it, they showed holes; the user preferred this, 2026-11-19) - turned at random, one mesh (`PlacedMesh`, so OptiX builds its tree). *diamonds*: the same packing with round brilliants (`MeshFill.BRILLIANT`: Tolkowsky's proportions, the table 53 %, crown 16.2 %, pavilion 43.1 % of the girdle's width, 64 triangles in eight-fold symmetry standing in for the real 57 facets); give them `-Drt.infill.ior=2.42`, diamond's - in the dark lamp scene they come out dark-faced and glinting, as diamonds do in a dark room (198 of them, 4096 spp, 53 s). *sponge* is a conversion of the surface, not a filling (`objects/LatticeFill`, the model itself is replaced): a soft thick foam of round uneven holes beside each other, after a reference photo, built by the Blender recipe the user gave (Cell Fracture or Voronoi, Inset Faces, Solidify, Subdivision Surface - or Houdini's VDB smoothing) as a distance field. The shell is every point within half `-Drt.infill.lattice.thickness` (3) wall widths of the triangles themselves - by the distance to dense samples laid on every triangle, their tangent plane near the surface (smooth corner normals blended per sample, which took faint stripes out), not to a voxel grid, so its sides are smooth and an open or badly wound file does as well as a closed one. The cells are a Voronoi diagram on the surface: seeds thinned to 0.9 pitches, then 3 passes of Lloyd, so they stay uneven; a hole is inset in each to leave a wall of 0.28 pitch (`.wall`), the holes' corners rounded by a polynomial smooth minimum of the two nearest cell boundaries (`.pore`, 0.35 pitch), and shell and walls are joined by a smooth maximum 0.17 pitch wide (`.round`, 0.6 wall widths) - oval holes, soft rounded walls, tunnels (matched side by side against the reference photo on `models/foam/patch.mjs`). The isosurface is taken by surface nets, streamed a layer of nodes at a time, as a smooth-shaded mesh wound outwards (glass depends on that). Sizes are fractions of the model height: `.pitch` (0.035), `.detail` (grid cells along a pitch, 22 - the fineness, and the cost: nodes grow with its cube); `-Drt.infill.lattice.log` prints the laps. The 0.5 x 0.5 test patch (pitch 0.1 of its height, 1 900 cells): 89 s and 8.5 M triangles. Dropped: a honeycomb of hexagonal holes (`lattice`, 14 Lloyd passes and hard bisector walls; removed 2026-11-20 at the user's request - a figure with fine folds lost them under it), two earlier sponges - balls cut out of a thin shell (a sieve) and deep round pits in the solid (a golf ball) - and a log-sum-exp smooth minimum over all boundaries, which shrank every hole. In the viewer every added object - a loaded model, the ball and the block alike - has a *Forma powierzchni* box (`PlacedObject.form`: bryła, gąbka, piramidki, diamenty; threads stay out, they are spheres and cannot ride the cheap placement): the converted mesh is built once on its own thread in the object's own proportions, the object shows as a solid meanwhile, and it is then placed, moved, turned and resized as any model; a change of the pores' spacing builds it again; the form is saved in the .rtscene (`object.N.form`, `object.N.lattice=pitch`; a file without one, or with the removed honeycomb, holds solids). The panel itself has not been clicked through - the build, placement and file are covered by `PlacedObjectFormTest`, `LatticeFillTest` and `SceneFileTest`. `-Drt.infill.count` caps how many (each size gets at least 50 000 throws, since a few large ones need many to find room: a ball of at most 200 large glass shards is `size=0.077 smallest=0.6 count=200`, 198 fitting); A *spheres* style (balls of many sizes, the large ones standing out of the outline) was tried and dropped (2026-11-19). `-Drt.infill.color=r,g,b` makes them matte in that colour instead of the model's material; `-Drt.infill.material=glass` makes them glass (`-Drt.infill.ior`, 1.5; tinted through the body by `-Drt.infill.color` if given) - give it `-Drt.depth=32`, light crosses many beads: the 1.62-unit ball of threads took 61 s at 2048 spp full size where matte took 17 s, of pyramids 48 s. For caustics under such a ball, studio-model takes `-Drt.studio.floor=matte[:r,g,b]` (the black mirror catches no photons), `-Drt.studio.lamp=x,y,z[,irradiance[,radius]]` (a small glowing ball on the light list, its brightness the light it casts on the figure's middle, 4 and 0.04 by default) and `-Drt.studio.softboxes=<k>` (dims the three softboxes); with `-Drt.studio.lamp=-2.5,4.5,-2.5,3 -Drt.studio.softboxes=0.1 -Drt.studio.floor=matte:0.4,0.4,0.42 -Drt.tonemap=aces -Drt.caustics=on` the light through the ball scatters into a field of sparks across its shadow in front of it (2048 spp: threads 125 s, pyramids 88 s, 12-13 % of the photons landing). The tumbler's dark look - a pool of light, a long shadow, black round it - comes from `-Drt.studio.wall=off -Drt.studio.softboxes=0` (zero leaves them out, not aimed at for nothing) and `-Drt.studio.lamp.cone=<degrees>` (a black tube of 16 panels and a back round the lamp, open towards the figure, so it lights a pool of that half-angle), with the camera placed by `-Drt.camera.from|at|vfov` as in the `file` scene: `-Drt.studio.floor=matte:0.33,0.32,0.31 -Drt.studio.lamp=-4,2.2,0,4,0.05 -Drt.studio.lamp.cone=22 -Drt.camera.from=2.5,5.5,4.5 -Drt.camera.at=1.6,0,0 -Drt.camera.vfov=40 -Drt.aspect=1.5625 -Drt.width=1600` (4096 spp: threads 87 s, pyramids 69 s). Glass pyramids show black and navy specks in the studio: their flat faces mirror the dark sky and wall, as a cut stone does in a dark room - under the bright sky of `model` the same ball has none, and 128 bounces change nothing against 32. `-Drt.studio.material=glass|diamond` makes the figure itself clear (1.5, or 2.42) instead of gold: `models/diamond/gen.mjs` (untracked) writes a round brilliant with the full 57 facets, z-up, rendered flat (`-Drt.smooth=off`, or the welded corners round it) at `-Drt.studio.height=1.2` in the same dark lamp scene - 110 triangles, 39 s at 4096 spp. `CausticMap` gives more than 64 casting spheres one aim sphere together: merging 52 k of them pair by pair would take n cubed. The beads are a `BvhNode` of `Sphere`s, the GPU's custom primitives: on the RTX 2060 through OptiX a 1.62-unit ball filled with 88 k beads of threads took 7.3 s to fill (8.6 M cells inside at 262 cubed) and 2.4 s to trace at 1080x1920, 256 spp, full size; Yennefer with threads (size 0.008, `res=384`) 43 k beads in 5.1 s, 2.1 s to trace. Thin parts thinner than a few cells (Yennefer's arms) come out sparse or empty: raise `res`, lower `size`. A turntable turns the filling as it turns the figure, but spheres are flattened and their tree built again every frame.

## Scenes and Sampling
Every scene from the three books lives in `scenes/BookScenes` as a named builder returning a
`SceneDefinition`; `-Drt.scene=<name>` picks one (`cornell-final`, `final-week`, `cornell-smoke`,
`perlin-spheres`, `quads`, …). `-Drt.samples`, `-Drt.width`,
`-Drt.depth` and `-Drt.aspect` scale and reframe any scene from the command line, since the books quote their finales at sample
counts that take hours, and `-Drt.out` redirects the render away from the committed `image.ppm`.

Shading follows The Rest of Your Life. A material returns a `ScatterRecord`: a diffuse surface
hands back a density to sample (`CosinePdf` around the normal, `SpherePdf` inside fog) while a
mirror or glass hands back the single ray it sends, and the renderer divides each sample by the
density it came from. A scene may also name `Camera.lights` - shapes worth aiming at - and then
half of every diffuse bounce's samples go straight at a light (`MixturePdf` over `HittablePdf`
and the surface's own density), which is what clears the noise out of a dim room; `-Drt.lights=off`
renders the same scene without it. `Quad` and `Sphere` implement `pdfValue`/`random` for this, and
the GPU carries the light list as bare geometry separate from the tree it traverses. Emissive
surfaces only emit through their front face.

## Build, Test, and Development Commands
- `mvn compile` — compiles Kotlin sources with the Kotlin Maven plugin targeting JVM 17.
- `mvn clean package` — recreates the shaded jar under `target/` and is the baseline for CI.
- `mvn exec:java` — runs `MainKt`, generating/overwriting `image.ppm`; commit only curated outputs.
- `mvn test` or `mvn -Dtest=CameraTest test` — executes unit tests (add them under `src/test/kotlin`).
- `GpuCpuComparisonTest` and `ImageRegressionTest` need a CUDA device and skip themselves without one. They compare against CPU renders stored before the CPU path tracer was removed; those references are frozen and cannot be rendered again, so a scene added to the comparison needs a reference made some other way. `UPDATE_REFERENCE_IMAGE` no longer exists.

## Coding Style & Naming Conventions
Follow idiomatic Kotlin style: 4-space indentation, `UpperCamelCase` for classes such as `HittableList`, `lowerCamelCase` for functions/properties, and `SCREAMING_SNAKE_CASE` only for constants. Keep packages under `rayTraceTypescript.*` to mirror directories. Favor `val` over `var`, keep mutable lists encapsulated (as in `HittableList`), and document non-obvious math near vector or material logic. Reuse helper extensions (e.g., `Vector.minus`) instead of ad-hoc calculations to preserve consistency.

## Testing Guidelines
Add tests beside the mirrored package in `src/test/kotlin`, using Kotlin Test or JUnit 5 so Maven Surefire can discover them. Name test classes `<Class>Test` and methods `fun rendersBlurredBackground()` so failures read naturally. Test deterministic pieces (vector math, material scatter logic, random helpers with seeded RNG) and gate image regressions by comparing computed pixel statistics rather than raw ppm bytes. Run `mvn test` locally before pushing; target coverage that hits every material branch at least once.

## Commit & Pull Request Guidelines
Git history shows short imperative messages (`add .gitignore`, `finished ray tracing in one weekend`), so keep titles under ~72 characters, starting with a verb (`tune camera depth`). Each PR should describe the scene/camera changes, list commands run (e.g., `mvn clean package`), attach before/after crops of `image.ppm` when visuals change, and link tracking issues. Avoid bundling refactors with feature additions; open follow-up tickets for larger rewrites.

## Rendering Output & Assets
Generated `image.ppm` files can be large; ignore intermediate renders and only commit canonical samples referenced in docs. When tweaking `camera` defaults (aspect ratio, samples, depth), capture the rationale in PR notes so reviewers understand performance vs. fidelity trade-offs. Store environment-specific configs (e.g., higher sample settings) in local profiles rather than changing `Main.kt` defaults.

Every render writes a PNG beside its PPM (`render/PpmWriter`, same pixels); the PPM, the PNG, the viewer and films all turn linear light into 8 bits through `render/ToneMap` - by default the books' square-root gamma with highlights clipped, `-Drt.tonemap=aces` the filmic curve games use (Narkowicz's ACES fit, then sRGB), `-Drt.exposure=<EV>` scaling the light first (the `file` scene defaults to ACES). A camera may carry an HDR map round the scene (`Camera.environment`, `textures/Environment` reads Radiance .hdr and halves it to at most 2048 wide; `-Drt.env=<file.hdr>`, `-Drt.env.intensity`, `-Drt.env.turn=<degrees>` in the `file` scene): escaping rays take their colour from it in both engines (`sky_colour`, the map uploaded once per session by `gpu/EnvironmentOnCard`), with no importance sampling - the scene's sun sphere still carries the direct light. Sun Temple's own skybox, turned 137 degrees so its sun lines up with the FBX's directional light, at 8 times its values with the sun at 3 (`-Drt.sun.power`), gives its NVIDIA screenshots' look; `-Drt.pfm` adds the unclipped linear pixels as a PFM. Renders started from `Main` are denoised by default (`-Drt.denoise=off` disables it): beside the raw image they write `<name>-denoised.ppm/.png`: after the image, the kernel renders the two guides, first-hit albedo and normal, in one pass (`GpuSession.MODE_GUIDES`: `AOV_MODE 1` with `GUIDES`, which writes the normal into an output of its own; 256 spp) and a denoiser behind `render/ImageDenoiser` cleans it. That is NVIDIA's OptiX denoiser (`gpu/optix/OptixDenoiser`, the AOV model, normals in world space, its weights come with the driver) on the card that traces (`GpuRenderer.requestedDenoiser`); a second card is not used. It needs the OptiX headers like the OptiX engine: without them (`native/optix/fetch.ps1`) renders come out undenoised, with a message. On a Yennefer frame at 1080x1920 and 64 spp it takes 0.07 s on the RTX 2060 (a GTX 1070 took 0.31 s, 0.26 s of it the denoiser, the rest the PCIe x4 copies, and gave the same image). Intel Open Image Denoise on the CPU, which denoised everything before 2026-11-15, was removed (2026-11-17): 1.34 s a frame on the four-core i5, and 33.3 dB against a 4096-spp render where OptiX scores 34.0 dB. Denoised stills and films trace at half the width and height by default (`-Drt.upscale=2`; `-Drt.upscale=off` renders at full size) (`CameraSetup.halved`: each pixel at the middle of a 2x2 block) and has OptiX's UPSCALE2X model denoise and enlarge in one call (`ImageDenoiser.upscale`), (the raw still is written at half size, the denoised one at full; the viewer ignores it). That model needs a Volta or newer card: on a GTX 1070 it returned a smeared image with ghost edges and no error (Cornell box 33.7 dB against 35.1 for bicubic enlarging, on the RTX 2060 40.5 dB), so a card older than that (`GpuRenderer.canUpscale`) denoises at full size. Yennefer at 64 spp: 0.19 s a film frame instead of 0.50 (trace 140 ms, guides 22, denoising 71 beside it), at 29.1-29.2 dB from the full-size render against 35.5 dB for full size vs a 4096-spp still - the gold's finest reflections, the face and the lacing, go soft.  Gathering the guides in the image's own pass, from its camera rays, was tried (2026-11-16) and dropped: on the RTX 2060 through OptiX it slowed the image by 81 ms a film frame (407 to 488 ms), more than the separate passes took (87 ms), even with nothing gathered (+28 ms) or the sums written straight to memory (+72 ms). Reads come back through page-locked host memory (`DeviceMemory.allocateHost`): 2 ms for a 1080x1920 image instead of 4.

## Turntable Films
A turntable film turns the scene's objects in place while the camera, the lights and the room stand still - what StlViewer's "Film obrotowy" looks like (it turns its camera with the lamps and wall instead). `-Drt.turntable=film.mp4` renders one from the command line (`gpu/TurntableRender`: the parts that are neither lights nor walls, or `-Drt.turntable.parts=a;b`, about the middle of their foot - the footprint of their lowest 5% of height (`gpu/Footprint`), as StlViewer turns a figure, so a raised sword or an outstretched arm does not pull the axis aside and set the base wandering; the viewer's film turns its objects the same way; `-Drt.turntable.seconds|turns|fps|reverse|quality=max`, `-Drt.samples`); the viewer's panel button *Film obrotowy…* opens `gui/TurntableDialog` (the selected object, else all added ones, else the scene's own), and `-Drt.gui.film=film.mp4` starts that path without clicks. `gpu/TurntableFilm` traces each frame in ~100 ms slices with its own seed and 32-sample guides from one pass, and denoises and writes it on a worker while the GPU traces the next - at full size the RTX 2060, tracing and denoising, takes Yennefer's frames back to back at 0.56 s a frame (trace 405 ms, guides 70 ms, the denoiser sharing the card); upscaled, as by default, at 0.16 s - and a film ends by printing where a frame's time went; `-Drt.turntable.degrees=<d>` turns by less than a full turn (a round brilliant looks the same turned by 45 degrees, so it loops as seamlessly, slower), and `-Drt.turntable.swing=on` swings instead: out to that angle and no further, easing in and out (half a cosine), the last frame at the angle itself - the outward half only, whose return is the same frames reversed in ffmpeg (`reverse,trim=start_frame=1:end_frame=N-1`, then concat), so a swing there and back costs half: the diamond, 151 frames at 60 fps, 2048 spp traced at 800x512 and upscaled, 6.1 s a frame, 15.5 min, joined into 300 frames that loop. `-Drt.temporal=on` denoises a film whose turning parts are all placed meshes with OptiX's temporal model (TEMPORAL_UPSCALE2X, or TEMPORAL_AOV at full size), each frame carried into the next along a flow guide the guides pass traces (`PlacedMesh.turning`, `FrameTurn`: a hit on a turning mesh turned back a frame and projected onto the still camera; 0.006 px from the CPU's on average), with the history primed on the turn's last 8 frames so the loop does not jump - off by default, since on the gold Yennefer in fog it halved the background's flicker at 16 spp but cost the figure 1 dB against 512 spp, and 2.3 dB at 64 spp: gold's highlights come from the still lamps and smear along the surface's flow (a trust map keeping the flow off metal saved the figure and made the background flicker more); `render/VideoEncoder` pipes the frames to ffmpeg (`-Drt.ffmpeg`, `FFMPEG` or the PATH; H.264 High, yuv420p, BT.709, key frame every 2 s, StlViewer's bitrates), renders one turn and copies it for the others, and writes `<name>.part.mp4` until the film is whole, so a cancelled or failed film leaves nothing. `gpu/Placement` folds a move of a `PlacedMesh` into its placement, so turning one costs a tree copy per frame, not a rebuild; `studio-model` (StlViewer's gold-reel studio, for comparing the two renderers) builds its figure that way.

## Interactive Viewer
`-Drt.gui` opens the chosen scene in a window (`gui/Viewer`) instead of rendering to a file: left drag orbits around the point the camera looks at, Shift + left drag or middle drag pans, the wheel or right drag dollies, Ctrl + click stands the selected model on the floor under the cursor, R resets, D toggles denoising, S asks for a file name (suggested `viewer-<scene>-<time>` beside `-Drt.out`) and saves PPM/PNG plus the denoised copy, Ctrl+S / Ctrl+O save and load the whole scene as a `.rtscene` text file (`gui/SceneFile`: base scene and its switches, camera, image settings, mode, lights and parts, each added object with its model path relative to the file; `-Drt.gui.load=file` opens one from the command line, picking its base scene), +/- change the field of view, Delete removes the selected model. The scene's own objects (`BuiltInObject`) move and turn like added ones - both are `Movable` - by standing them in `Translate(RotateY(...))` about their footprint and moving their light-sampling stand-in along; "Przywróć obiekty sceny" puts them back. Parts marked `isSurface` (the Cornell box walls) are `WallObject`s instead: fixed in place, their material editable (a changed wall is rebuilt as the same quad); added objects and walls share `HasMaterial`, and scene files keep changed walls as `wall.N.*`. The side panel (`gui/ControlPanel`) sets the resolution and the sample target and adds models from any format `MeshLoader` reads (`gui/PlacedObject`: position, turn, height, finish, z-up for 3D-printing files); `-Drt.gui.models=a.stl;b.obj` opens with models placed. A mesh may carry per-face materials (`Mesh.faceMaterials` into `Mesh.palette` of `MeshMaterial`, read from an OBJ's `mtllib`/`usemtl` and .mtl `Kd`, `Pm`, `Pr`, `Ke`, `d`, `Ni`); transforms keep them, `MeshBlock` carries them in leaf order, `PlacedMesh.palette` and `toColouredHittables` use them, and `cornell-model` keeps them unless `-Drt.model.material` is given (it also stands only STL/3MF up from z). It opens in the quick look for setting a scene up (kernel `AOV_MODE 3`: first hit shaded by its colour under a head light, glass seen through, 16 jittered rays per pixel at full size); T or the panel's *Tryb* switches to ray tracing, and `-Drt.gui.raytrace` opens in it. Object edits rebuild the GPU scene 80 ms after the last change, in the same `GpuSession` (`replaceScene` swaps the scene buffers and keeps the kernels while the feature switches match). A loaded model keeps a `gpu/MeshBlock` - its triangles and SAH tree built once in its own space - and is placed as a `PlacedMesh` (scale, turn about y, offset): the flattener copies that tree in transformed and grafts it into the scene's tree as one primitive, so moving, turning or resizing a 400k-triangle model costs ~130 ms instead of a ~2 s rebuild. In ray tracing, while the camera moves it renders as a turntable film does at its quickest: half size, 16 spp, guides with the flow the camera's move makes (`setCamera(setup, previous)`: the camera of the frame before rides in the camera buffer, so both engines trace it), enlarged by OptiX's temporal denoiser (`GpuRenderer.motionDenoiser`, TEMPORAL_UPSCALE2X on the RTX 2060) that carries each frame into the next, and denoised beside the tracing of the next frame - Yennefer at 1080x1920 in 10-12 frames a second, the RTX 2060 doing both (trace 20-35 ms, guides ~50, denoising ~75 as they share the card); without OptiX or with `-Drt.gui.motion=plain` it keeps the quarter-size 1-spp preview. `-Drt.gui.spin=<degrees a second>` keeps the camera orbiting to time that, and the log prints frames a second every 2 s. Once it holds still for 150 ms it accumulates full-size samples, denoising at 16, 64, 256… spp. The look is FlatLaf's dark theme (`com.formdev:flatlaf`, set up in `gui/Theme` before any component is made) with an amber accent; shortcuts go through a window-wide key dispatcher, so they work whatever holds the focus except text fields. It shares `gpu/GpuSession` with the file renderer: scene buffers uploaded once, one kernel per mode, the camera and resolution passed per launch, and output buffers for the denoiser guides.
